### 1. What is Protected Health Information (PHI)?
PHI is any individually identifiable health information that is created or received by a health care provider, health plan, public health authority, employer, life insurer, school or university, or health care clearinghouse and relates to the past, present, or future physical or mental health or condition of an individual; the provision of health care to an individual; or the past, present, or future payment for the provision of health care to an individual.

### 2. What are some examples of PHI?
Examples of PHI include names, addresses, birth dates, Social Security numbers, medical record numbers, and photographs.

### 3. What are the 18 identifiers that make health information PHI?
The 18 identifiers are:
1. Names
2. All geographical subdivisions smaller than a state
3. All elements of dates (except year) for dates directly related to an individual
4. Phone numbers
5. Fax numbers
6. Email addresses
7. Social Security numbers
8. Medical record numbers
9. Health plan beneficiary numbers
10. Account numbers
11. Certificate/license numbers
12. Vehicle identifiers and serial numbers, including license plate numbers
13. Device identifiers and serial numbers
14. Web Universal Resource Locators (URLs)
15. Internet Protocol (IP) address numbers
16. Biometric identifiers, including finger and voice prints
17. Full face photographic images and any comparable images
18. Any other unique identifying number, characteristic, or code

### 4. Is all health information considered PHI?
No, not all health information is considered PHI. Health information that does not identify an individual and with respect to which there is no reasonable basis to believe that the information can be used to identify an individual is not considered PHI.

### 5. What is de-identified health information?
De-identified health information is health information that has been stripped of all 18 identifiers. De-identified health information is not subject to the HIPAA Privacy Rule.

### 6. Can PHI be used or disclosed for marketing purposes?
PHI cannot be used or disclosed for marketing purposes without the individual’s authorization.

### 7. Can PHI be used or disclosed for research purposes?
PHI can be used or disclosed for research purposes, but only if the covered entity obtains either the individual’s authorization or a waiver of authorization from an Institutional Review Board (IRB) or Privacy Board.

### 8. What is the Minimum Necessary Standard?
The Minimum Necessary Standard requires covered entities to make reasonable efforts to limit the use or disclosure of, and requests for, PHI to the minimum necessary to accomplish the intended purpose.

### 9. Does the Minimum Necessary Standard apply to all uses and disclosures?
No, the Minimum Necessary Standard does not apply to disclosures to or requests by a health care provider for treatment purposes, disclosures to the individual who is the subject of the information, or uses or disclosures made pursuant to an individual’s authorization.

### 10. Can a patient’s family member or friend pick up a prescription for them?
Yes, a pharmacist may use their professional judgment to disclose PHI to a patient’s family member, friend, or other person who is involved in the patient’s care.

### 11. Can a covered entity disclose PHI to law enforcement?
A covered entity may disclose PHI to law enforcement officials in certain circumstances, such as to report a crime on the covered entity’s premises or to identify or locate a suspect, fugitive, material witness, or missing person.

### 12. Can a covered entity disclose PHI to a public health authority?
A covered entity may disclose PHI to a public health authority that is authorized by law to collect or receive such information for the purpose of preventing or controlling disease, injury, or disability.

### 13. What is a limited data set?
A limited data set is PHI that excludes certain direct identifiers. A limited data set may be used and disclosed for research, health care operations, and public health purposes, provided that the recipient enters into a data use agreement with the covered entity.

### 14. What is the difference between PHI and ePHI?
ePHI is electronic protected health information. It is PHI that is created, received, maintained, or transmitted in electronic form.

### 15. How long is PHI protected?
PHI is protected for 50 years after the individual’s death.

### 16. Can a patient request a copy of their PHI?
Yes, a patient has the right to inspect and obtain a copy of their PHI in a designated record set.

### 17. Can a patient request an amendment to their PHI?
Yes, a patient has the right to request an amendment of their PHI in a designated record set if they believe the information is inaccurate or incomplete.

### 18. Can a patient request restrictions on the use and disclosure of their PHI?
Yes, a patient has the right to request restrictions on the use and disclosure of their PHI for treatment, payment, or health care operations.

### 19. What should a covered entity do if they receive a request for PHI from a third party?
A covered entity should verify the identity and authority of the person requesting the PHI and ensure that the disclosure is permitted by the HIPAA Privacy Rule.

### 20. What are the penalties for a wrongful disclosure of PHI?
The penalties for a wrongful disclosure of PHI can include civil monetary penalties and, in some cases, criminal penalties.
